The 9 metrics

Methodology

Each metric is converted to a 0–100 score by ranking today's value against a rolling 20–30 year window of its own history (point-in-time, no lookahead) — the market is compared against the last generation, not against 1955, so the score adapts to structural regime shifts. Slow valuation metrics (CAPE, trailing P/E, household allocation, Buffett Indicator) blend the level percentile (60%) with the 3-year rate-of-change percentile (40%): bubbles are expensive and accelerating — 1993 was expensive but calm, 1999 was both, and the index tells them apart. The equity risk premium is inverted (low premium = expensive). Margin debt blends its detrended level (50%) with its 12-month growth rate (50%), because bubbles show up in the acceleration of leverage.


The composite is a weighted average, renormalized over available metrics — if a source goes stale (no update within 2× its normal cadence), it is dropped rather than allowed to freeze the index. Weights were tuned against ten historical episodes (1982, 1987, 1993, 1995, 1999–2000, 2004, 2007, 2009, 2013, 2021), blended 50/50 with structural priors and capped, so no single metric can dominate. The headline number is smoothed over 3 months. The unweighted median of the 9 scores is shown as a robustness check: a large gap between weighted and median means a few heavy metrics drive the reading.

What this is not: a timing tool. Valuation metrics are poor short-term timers — the market stayed "overvalued" from 1996 to 2000. Read the score as "how stretched are conditions versus history," not "sell now." Nothing here is investment advice.
Data provenance: CAPE, P/E, P/S, S&P 500 prices (multpl.com); GDP, 10-year Treasury, corporate equities market value, household equity allocation (FRED); current margin debt (FINRA, last 13 months); current top-10 weight (Slickcharts).
